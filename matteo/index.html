Sea to Teide to Sea

Tenerife Bluetrail 110K, 2028 edition. From Playa de los Cristianos at sea level, up to La Rambleta on Teide at 3555 m — the cable-car top station, not the summit — and back down to the Atlantic at Puerto de la Cruz. 110 km, 6250 m of climbing, and the same again of descent.

⚠ Blood tests — before B1 (16 November)

The most urgent item on this page. Ask for ferritin + transferrin saturation + CRP together (ferritin on its own cannot be read in someone who trains: it rises with inflammation and can look normal over empty stores), plus a full blood count, TSH, glucose, creatinine, liver enzymes, lipids, B12 and vitamin D. The reference manual adds cortisol and testosterone to its fatigue panel: ask the doctor, it is their call.

How: in the morning, fasted, hydrated and not ill, after a rest or easy day, and with no long effort, long descent or hard session in the 2-3 days before — ferritin and CRP stay raised for days after exertion and with any infection, and a "normal" ferritin taken that way can hide empty stores; dehydration inflates haemoglobin and haematocrit. Keep the reports: the reference that matters is your own trend, always in the same conditions.

If you have started creatine, write it on the request — it raises creatinine without the kidneys being involved. Then the values into /log, and any iron supplementation is the doctor's decision on the result, never a low number on its own.

Later — five proposals, your call · none is on the calendar yet

They came out of the endurance nutrition manual (15 Sep 2026). They are not commitments like the ones above: each has its moment, months or years ahead. Say yes or no whenever you like, in /log; the ones you accept enter the plan at their date.

  1. The sweat test — once in the cool (B1-B2), once in the heat (B3). How: 60-90 minutes at race effort; weigh yourself naked before and after (toilet first, towel off properly), note how much you drink, no peeing in between. Weight lost + fluid drunk = sweat lost; 1 kg ≈ 1 litre. What it is for: how much water to carry between two water points, how much salt you need, and a check — if you finish a long effort heavier than you started, you drank too much. It is not an amount to drink: you always drink to thirst. Log it like this: sweat 1.1 L/h · 14 °C · 75 min.
  2. Vitamin C around the race and every altitude trip. ~500 mg a day for one or two weeks around a very hard effort: in people under intense physical stress (marathoners, skiers) it halved colds. Not all year round — taken continuously at high doses, antioxidant vitamins can blunt the adaptation to training.
  3. Ferritin, before sleeping high in race week. At altitude the body makes red cells and needs iron to do it: with low stores the response is weaker. The B0 bloods (the box at the top of this page) give the starting point; they are repeated near B6, in the same conditions. Any iron is the doctor's decision on the result.
  4. The freezer test for every race food — before the B3 night rehearsals. Everything you will carry on the high, cold section spends a few hours in the freezer: bars and gels at −5 °C turn to stones. What you cannot bite does not go in the cold-section bag.
  5. The sheet for whoever assists you at the three assistance points — with the race card, in B6. Written by you a few days before: expected times, what to have ready, what you leave carrying. A few fixed questions at every point (what have you eaten and drunk since the last one? the salt? what do you want now?); checking bottles and wrappers instead of trusting the answer; sweet and salty, you choose. And no drop-out is decided at an assistance point: you eat, you leave, and it is discussed again at the next one.

Hours and long effort never rise in the same week, and no step exceeds 10% — both enforced by the generator, which refuses a load ramp that would break them. The 10% figure is a deliberate safety margin, not an evidence-based threshold, and it is worth knowing which: it has been tested and it did not work. A randomised trial put novice runners on a graded 10%-per-week programme or let them progress as they liked and found no difference in injury rate; the real signal in the data is avoiding sudden jumps above ~30%, which sits well inside this ceiling anyway. The number itself comes from 1980s running books, not a study. Kept because over 83 weeks a conservative ceiling costs you almost nothing.

Read the “longest D−” column as pulses, not a ramp. It hits full magnitude every third build week — 500 · 1000 · 1400 here — sits at half in between, and is zero in down weeks. It used to rise every single week and hold near maximum, which in a twenty-week block is about eight consecutive weeks of near-maximal eccentric load. The repeated-bout effect is exactly why that buys nothing: protection from one big descent lasts weeks, not days. And it is the one quality you cannot cross-train around, so an injury there ends the race rather than costing a session.

Where in the day matters as much as how much. Climb the height over five or six hours, then descend it unbroken. Driving to the top and descending on fresh legs meets the number and misses the adaptation — the race gives you its descent on hour-13 legs, after a night awake.

What the efforts mean

This plan prescribes duration and effort, never a pace and never a heart rate. Heart rate lies at hour eight of a long day, and pace is meaningless on ground that swings from runnable to hands-on-knees. So the whole system rests on one instrument — and reading it accurately is a skill, not a given.

RPEThe talk test — say it out loudWhat it is for
2–3Full conversation. You could keep going all day.Recovery, shakeouts, easy spins
4Full sentences, aware of your breathing.The bulk of the plan — easy runs and long runs both
5Sentences, but you would rather not.Steady. Long-run finishes, climbs
6–7Short phrases only.Tempo, sustained hills. Once or twice a week
8–9A word or two.Short hill reps. Rare, never in the last three weeks
So "easy 55′ RPE 3-4" means: fifty-five minutes at a pace where you could hold a conversation the whole way. If you cannot say a full sentence out loud, you are running the wrong session — slow down. That is not a soft target: roughly 85% of your weekly hours belong at 3-4, and the commonest way a plan like this fails is easy days creeping to 5 because you feel good. A week that is uniformly medium is too hard to recover from and too easy to adapt to.

Four ways to check yourself

  • The talk test. Out loud, not "I think I could". It separates 4 from 5.
  • Could you have run it again? At the end of an easy run, honestly. The best check for long runs.
  • Next morning's legs. The strongest signal. An "RPE 4" that leaves your legs at 5/10 was not an RPE 4, whatever it felt like at the time.
  • Nasal breathing. Crude, but if you can breathe through your nose only, you are genuinely easy.

Two scales — never mix them

  • Running RPE asks how hard is this to sustain? — the talk test, above.
  • Gym RPE asks something else entirely: how close did that set come to the point where you physically could not do another rep?
  • Reps in reserve is the answer — how many more you could still have done with good form when you put the weight down. Reps where the form falls apart do not count.
  • Then RPE = 10 − reps in reserve. The table below spells it out.
Gym RPEReps you could still have doneWhere it is used

What “3 × 8 at RPE 6” actually instructs. Do eight reps. At the end of the eighth, ask honestly — could I have done four more, with the same form?

Yes, roughly four more → correct weight, keep it.  ·  Only one or two more → that was RPE 8-9, too heavy; take load off the next set.  ·  Ten more, easily → too light; add load, or slow the lowering to 3-4 seconds.

So it is a load-selection instruction, not an effort rating. The reps are fixed by the programme; the RPE tells you which weight to use. It is the only honest way to prescribe load when nobody knows your one-rep max and your kit is what it is.
The trap: the same number means opposite things on the two scales. Running RPE 6 is hard — short phrases only. Gym RPE 6 is comparatively easy — four reps still in the tank. Carry the running meaning into the gym and you will go far too heavy on day one. They are separate fields in the log for exactly this reason.

And expect to misjudge it at first. Estimating reps-in-reserve is a skill, unreliable until you have been close enough to failure a few times to have something to compare against. Until then err lighter: in the technique phase the movement pattern is the goal, and a weight 20% too light costs nothing, while one 20% too heavy costs your form.

The full rule, with the anchor sessions and the drift detector: knowledge/calibration-rpe.md

Expect to misread it at first, and expect specific distortions. Fresh legs make you underrate. Running with other people almost always makes you underrate. Heat, cold, wind, altitude and terrain all mean the same effort comes at a slower pace — that is the scale working, not failing, and it is exactly why it suits a race that runs from a warm beach to 3555 m. Stress and poor sleep genuinely raise the effort of the same run; when that happens the rating is not wrong, and the plan should bend rather than override it.

One exception, and it is the one that matters on Teide: above about 2500 m the scale itself breaks. Everything above is the same reading arriving at a slower pace. Thin air is different — it raises your breathing at any effort, and the talk test is a breathing test, so it reads two or more points high from roughly 2500 m up. Obey it literally and you crawl the climb to the summit; ignore it and you blow up. Up there the anchor becomes steps per breath, set lower down and carried up, and “could I hold this for another hour?” Three to five hours of this race run on a distorted instrument, and effort is the only instrument here — so it goes on the race card.

Settled, and still open

Decided

  • The taper drops volume and keeps intensity — and race week carries one short sharp effort. 4-6 × 20-30 s at RPE 6-7 inside an easy run, about three days out, full recovery. Not a session, and it costs no measurable fatigue. Two meta-analyses agree the taper that works cuts volume 41-60% over 8-14 days and leaves intensity and frequency alone: volume is what causes the fatigue, intensity is what preserves the adaptation, and turning both down together is how a taper arrives rested and flat. Freshness is not sharpness. Still RPE 6-7 and never 8-9 — "maintain intensity" means keep doing the quality you were already doing, not introduce something harder.
  • A taper now exists, and it tapers. The generator first had none at all — your two hardest weeks of 83 would have been race week and the week before. The first fix then put a 3¾-hour long run in race week. Volume now falls 13 h 54′ → 9 h 42′ → 7 h 36′ → 3 h and the long effort falls faster, to an hour.
  • Poles: reopened, not settled. A “no” from someone who has never systematically trained with them, given 83 weeks out, is an untested preference rather than a decision — and 57 m/km with a huge continuous descent is exactly where poles help most, by sparing the quads for the descent this plan worries about above all. Re-confirm after a real trial before the specific block. Note the trap: the plan compensates for no-poles with more eccentric quad work, while its own strength file says your home setup cannot properly load the posterior chain or soleus. It removed the aid and cannot fully supply the substitute.
  • Tell it the truth, including the awkward parts. A usability review found this system had been punishing honest answers — a managed niggle lost you your plan, ordinary post-long-run soreness got escalated to "stop running", and saying "prefer not to say" about food got announced back at you in capitals. All three are fixed. A grumbly Achilles is now carried as a watch flag and the plan is built around it; sore calves after your longest run in months get called what they are, which is normal. Nothing you report honestly should cost you anything — if it does, that is a bug in the system, not a fact about you. Say so.
  • Bloods need three markers, not one. Ferritin alone is uninterpretable in a training athlete — it rises with inflammation and can read normal over empty stores. Order ferritin + transferrin saturation + CRP together, plus B12 and vitamin D, a full count, thyroid, glucose, kidney, liver and lipids. Once, cheap, and the only baseline you will ever have.
  • No weight loss. At 63 kg and 176 cm you are already lean, you are under-eating for the load ahead, and ferritin has never been measured. Fuelling goes up instead.
  • Strength at home — and it runs out in week 5. Measured 7 Sep: one 16 kg kettlebell and a 5 kg pack (corrected 22 Sep — it had been written as 18). That is 21 kg total, 33% of body mass, plus bands, a pull-up bar and two 1 kg ankle weights. It was recorded as “dumbbells”, plural, which hid the problem. Fine for technique. But from week 5, when lifting goes heavy at 4–6 reps, an 18 kg goblet squat is a warm-up rather than a stimulus — and the soleus, the second priority on this list, takes multiples of body weight in gait, so 23 kg cannot touch it. The free fix: load the pack. 5 kg is what is in it, not what it holds — sand, plates, water, books. Take it to 15–20 kg and it becomes the adjustable load the setup lacks, and it doubles as loaded steep uphill walking, which is both the best soleus work available and race-specific besides. After that: one second-hand 24–32 kg bell, or a gym block during the heavy phases for a real calf machine. The terrain is the actual answer — D+700 fifteen minutes away beats anything on a kitchen floor.

The daily loop

Open your card, train, fill in the form at the bottom, press Copy, paste it into Claude and press Enter. That is all.

Everything else happens inside that paste: tomorrow's card is written, the rest of the week re-plans itself, the week is reviewed when it ends, and if the plan itself needs rebuilding you are asked once — answer yes, in the form or in the reply, and it is done for you. You never type a command for any of it.

Tap what fits, skip what you like. A question you skip is sent as “?”, and that is a fine answer: at most one short question comes back, and nothing waits on it. On an ordinary day, Done as proposed plus the morning scales is a handful of taps. Every scale reads the same way — higher is better: legs 10 = fresh, sleep 5 = slept well, motivation 5 = keen, life outside training 5 = relaxed.
Skipped it, or something hurts? That is a log too. Tap Skipped, or Anything hurt? → Yes and where. You never have to invent a session to report a problem, and reporting one costs you nothing — a managed niggle is carried as a flag and the plan is built around it, not withheld. If telling the truth ever costs you something here, that is a bug in the system, not a fact about you. Say so.
Log the rest days too. A week of flat legs on full rest tells the plan more than any single run does — and a session you skipped and mentioned gets handled, while one you skipped silently breaks the following block. Nothing here nags you: it is pull-only by design, which also means it goes quiet and stale if you stop talking to it.

Plain words still work: anything you type after /log is read just as before, in Italian or English. /today and /week are there if you want them; you do not need them.

Rest between sets — part of the prescription, not a detail

The rest is what makes a strength session a strength session. Cut it and heavy low-rep work quietly becomes muscular endurance — which your running already trains, and which is the whole thing the programme is built to avoid. "4 × 5 heavy" at 45 seconds and at 3 minutes are different sessions.

WorkRestWhy
Heavy main lift 4-6 reps, RPE 7-82-3 minNeural, not metabolic. Full recovery is the point — the next set must be as good as the last.
Technique phase 3 × 8, RPE 6 — where you are now60-90 sSubmaximal by design, nothing to failure, so the pattern is the limiter rather than recovery.
Eccentric step-downs90 sDeliberate muscle damage; control is the limiter, and control goes first.
Calf work, standing and seated60-90 sHigh reps, small muscle, quick turnaround.
Isometric holds to fatigue60-90 sLong enough that the next hold is a hold, not a struggle.
Core and carries45-60 sNot the session's limiter; they finish it rather than drive it.
Rest is per set — including between sides. A single-leg RDL is two sets, not one, and it is easy to lose half the rest without noticing.

Rest longer if the form drifts, never shorter to save time. A session that will not fit has too many exercises, not rests that are too long — drop one and say which.
Not active for you: the upper-body block. It loads only on poles: yes — pull-ups, overhead triceps extension, dead hangs, farmer's carries, for the lats, triceps and grip that double-poling actually uses. Listed in exercise-library.md because the poles decision is reopened, not settled, and if it flips this block comes with it.
Loaded single-leg RDL is withdrawn — 10 Sep 2026. On the 10th you felt a small pull low on the right side of your back during the fourth set-block of Strength B, the bell (16 kg) in one hand, and stopped the exercise. That is a one-sided event, so it is carried as a watch flag rather than waved through as soreness.

The substitute keeps the pattern and drops the side. Your kit is one 16 kg bell, so the load cannot be reduced by picking a lighter weight — the variable that can change is which side it hangs on. One-handed loading adds a sideways and rotational demand on the lumbar spine that a two-handed hinge simply does not have. So until it clears: bell in both hands, rear toe lightly down for balance (a "kickstand" RDL), or bodyweight single-leg hinge if even that talks back. The suitcase carry — the same session's other one-sided load — drops to two sets and is the first thing out if anything twinges.

A second episode at the same site means no running and the bike instead, so there is nothing to be gained by testing it. And pain that radiates into the buttock or down the leg, numbness, pins and needles, or a leg that feels weak is not a training question at all — that is a doctor, the same week.
One warning that applies to all of them. Nothing goes to failure, ever — a failed set costs a run day and that trade is never worth it. In the technique phase, RPE 6 means four reps left in the tank: lifting RPE is 10 minus reps in reserve, which is not the talk-test scale you use running. Never mix the two.
